# Research-first outreach

This is the exact method Michael Isaac used on 31 August and 1 September 2026 to reach 20 independent consultants he had never met. Two cohorts of 10. The first cohort produced one booked research call inside 18 hours. Every email was written by an agent from the prospect's own website, reviewed by a human, and sent from a normal mailbox at 10 a day.

The method works because the ask is real. You research, you deliver what you promised, and the relationship follows. If the user cannot name a research question they would run even with no sales upside, stop and help them find one before building a list.

## What you produce

1. A research page (one public URL) that says who the user is, what they are researching, and what a participant gets.
2. A qualified list: 10 to 30 people with a name, an email from their own site, and one sentence that proves you read their site.
3. One email per person, assembled from the fixed template in [references/email-template.md](references/email-template.md). Only the first sentence changes.
4. A tracker row per send, per reply, and per booking. See [references/tracking.md](references/tracking.md).
5. After each call, the promised brief inside 24 hours.

## Step 0: preconditions

Confirm all five before writing a single email. Ask the user for what is missing.

- A research question the user would answer even if nobody ever bought anything. Michael's: how do independent consultants handle recurring client workflows that existing software does not support well?
- A research page. Outline in [references/research-page.md](references/research-page.md). A Substack post or a Notion page works if it is public and has the same sections.
- A scheduling link (Outlook Bookings, cal.com, Calendly).
- A mailbox on a domain the user owns, with a real postal address for the footer. No bulk senders, no purchased lists.
- A sample of the deliverable, if one exists. Michael added a one-page sample brief PDF to cohort 2. Optional for the first cohort.

## Step 1: define the target shape

You are looking for people who publish how they work. A consultant who describes a named process on their site has already told you what to ask about, and your first sentence will show you read it.

Qualify in:

- Independent, or a firm small enough that the founder answers the mail.
- A page that names a repeatable client workflow: an assessment, a diagnostic, a phased method, a named engagement type, a coverage offer.
- A person's name and an email address on the site. A contact form only is a weaker signal; use it as a last resort.

Qualify out:

- Agencies with a sales team.
- Sites with no named human.
- Sites with no process description. A list of services is not a workflow.
- Anyone the user already knows. Warm contacts get a different note.

Roles Michael used, for the mix: fractional COO, CFO, CTO, CMO, product leader, operations consultant, HR consultant, compliance consultant, healthcare-practice consultant, financial-services consultant.

## Step 2: build the list

Follow [references/list-building.md](references/list-building.md). Summary:

1. Run search queries per role and per industry. Look for the words consultants use for their own process: "assessment", "diagnostic", "our process", "how we work", "phase", "readiness", "coverage", "interim".
2. Open each site. Read the services page and the about page. Do not rely on the search snippet.
3. Record one row per person with the fields in the reference file.
4. Write the observation sentence while the page is open.
5. Source two to three times the number you intend to email. Half will fail qualification.

## Step 3: the observation sentence

One sentence. It must contain a specific noun from their site, a number or a named artifact if the site has one, and nothing evaluative. No "impressive", no "love what you are doing". If a stranger could have written it without opening the site, rewrite it.

Real examples from the run, with names removed:

- "I saw that your firm offers planned product-leadership coverage for parental leave, including a clean handoff when the product manager returns."
- "I saw that your 20-minute Leak Check produces a profitability scorecard, tax strategy, and enterprise-value gap report."
- "I noticed your fractional operations work often begins with an operational health check before process mapping, SOP design, and deeper implementation."
- "Your onboarding moves from consultation into a Financial Assessment of job costing, WIP, and cash flow before month-to-month CFO support."
- "Your SOC 2 and FDA Software Readiness Assessment is a clear example of a repeatable diagnostic that turns regulated context into a concrete plan."

## Step 4: assemble the email

Use the template verbatim. Change the greeting, the observation sentence, and nothing else unless the user changes the template for everyone. Pick one call-to-action variant per cohort so you can compare:

- Variant A, direct booking: link the scheduling page.
- Variant B, reply-first: "If you're open to it, just reply yes and I'll send the scheduling link."

Michael's cohort 1 ran variant B wording with a booking page on the research site. Cohort 2 ran variant B plus a link to the sample brief. Both are in the template file.

Subject line, unchanged for everyone: `Question about your client workflow`.

## Step 5: send

- 10 per day, from the user's own mailbox, one message per recipient. No CC, no BCC, no mail-merge tool.
- The user reads every email before it goes. Show them the batch as a table: name, company, observation sentence, variant.
- The footer carries the postal address and the opt-out line. Keep it on every message.
- Log each send in the tracker the same minute.

## Step 6: replies and bookings

- "Yes" reply: send the scheduling link within the hour. One line, no new pitch.
- Booking: build a one-page interview guide for the user, not for the participant. One goal: leave the call with one recurring workflow described well enough to write a one-page brief. Trigger, cadence, actors, tools in order, top two failure points, what they have tried, their words for "fixed". Do not pitch on the call.
- "No" reply: mark closed. Do not follow up.
- After the call: brief inside 24 hours. Then every other promise made on the call, in the order promised.

## Step 7: follow-up

One follow-up per person, five business days after the first send, only if it adds something: a link to the first aggregated finding, or the sample brief if the first email did not include it. Then stop. Two touches total.

## Results from the run, so you can set expectations

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Emails sent | 20, in two cohorts of 10 (31 Aug and 1 Sep 2026) |
| Replies from cohort 1 inside 18 hours | 1 |
| Calls booked from that reply | 1, scheduled the next morning |
| Deliverable sent after the call | Brief plus a themes memo plus this skill |

One reply in ten is the number to plan around for a first cohort with a real research question.

## If the user is selling, not researching

The method still applies when the user's real goal is future clients, on one condition: the research has to be real and the brief has to be delivered. A fractional product leader who wants engagements can run "how do product teams cover a PM's leave?" as an honest research question, interview ten heads of product, send each a brief, and be the obvious call when the leave happens. The sale arrives on the relationship's timeline, which for fractional work is often years. Do not shorten the timeline by pitching on the research call. The participant will hear a cold pitch with extra steps.
