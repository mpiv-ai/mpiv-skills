# Research-first outreach

Michael Isaac used this method on 31 August and 1 September 2026 to email 20 independent consultants he had never met. He sent two cohorts of 10. The first cohort produced one booked research call within 18 hours. An agent wrote each email from the prospect's own website. A person reviewed every email. The emails went out from a normal mailbox, 10 a day.

The ask is real. You are doing research. You deliver the brief you promised. Some of the people you interview become clients later. If the user cannot name a research question they would run with no chance of a sale, stop and help them find one before building a list.

## What you produce

1. A research page. One public URL that says who the user is, what they are researching, and what a participant gets.
2. A qualified list: 10 to 30 people, each with a name, an email from their own site, and one sentence that shows you read their site.
3. One email per person, from the fixed template in [references/email-template.md](references/email-template.md). Only the first sentence changes.
4. A tracker row for every send, reply, and booking. See [references/tracking.md](references/tracking.md).
5. After each call, the promised brief within 24 hours.

## Step 0: preconditions

Confirm all five before writing an email. Ask the user for whatever is missing.

- A research question the user would answer even if nobody ever bought anything. Michael's question: how do independent consultants handle recurring client workflows that existing software does not support well?
- A research page. Outline in [references/research-page.md](references/research-page.md). A Substack post or a Notion page works if it is public and has the same sections.
- A scheduling link (Outlook Bookings, cal.com, Calendly).
- A mailbox on a domain the user owns, with a real postal address for the footer. No bulk senders. No purchased lists.
- A sample of the deliverable, if one exists. Michael added a one-page sample brief PDF in cohort 2. Optional for the first cohort.

## Step 1: define the target

Look for people who describe how they work on their own site. A consultant who names a process on their site has told you what to ask about. Your first sentence will quote it back.

Include:

- Independent people, or firms small enough that the founder answers the mail.
- Sites with a page that names a repeatable client process: an assessment, a diagnostic, a phased method, a named engagement type, a coverage offer.
- A person's name and an email address on the site. A contact form only is weaker. Use it last.

Exclude:

- Agencies with a sales team.
- Sites with no named person.
- Sites with no process description. A list of services is not a process.
- Anyone the user already knows. Warm contacts get a different note.

Roles Michael used: fractional COO, CFO, CTO, CMO, product leader; operations, HR, compliance, healthcare-practice, and financial-services consultants.

## Step 2: build the list

Follow [references/list-building.md](references/list-building.md). In short:

1. Run search queries for each role and industry. Add the words consultants use for their own process: "assessment", "diagnostic", "our process", "how we work", "phase", "readiness", "coverage", "interim".
2. Open each site. Read the services page and the about page. Do not rely on the search snippet.
3. Record one row per person, with the fields in the reference file.
4. Write the observation sentence while the page is open.
5. Find two to three times as many candidates as you plan to email. About half will fail the checks.

## Step 3: the observation sentence

One sentence. It contains a specific noun from their site, plus a number or a named artifact if the site has one. It contains no opinion. No "impressive". No "love what you are doing". If a stranger could have written it without opening the site, rewrite it.

Sentences from the run, with names removed:

- "I saw that your firm offers planned product-leadership coverage for parental leave, including a clean handoff when the product manager returns."
- "I saw that your 20-minute Leak Check produces a profitability scorecard, tax strategy, and enterprise-value gap report."
- "I noticed your fractional operations work often begins with an operational health check before process mapping, SOP design, and deeper implementation."
- "Your onboarding moves from consultation into a Financial Assessment of job costing, WIP, and cash flow before month-to-month CFO support."
- "Your SOC 2 and FDA Software Readiness Assessment is a clear example of a repeatable diagnostic that turns regulated context into a concrete plan."

## Step 4: assemble the email

Use the template word for word. Change the greeting and the observation sentence. Change nothing else unless the user changes the template for everyone. Use one call-to-action variant per cohort so you can compare:

- Variant A: link the scheduling page.
- Variant B: "If you're open to it, just reply yes and I'll send the scheduling link."

Cohort 1 used variant B. Cohort 2 used variant B plus a link to the sample brief. Both are in the template file.

Subject line for everyone: `Question about your client workflow`.

## Step 5: send

- 10 per day, from the user's own mailbox, one message per recipient. No CC, no BCC, no mail-merge tool.
- The user reads every email before it goes. Show the batch as a table: name, company, observation sentence, variant.
- Keep the postal address and the opt-out line in the footer of every message.
- Log each send in the tracker right away.

## Step 6: replies and bookings

- "Yes" reply: send the scheduling link within the hour. One line. No new pitch.
- Booking: write a one-page interview guide for the user, not for the participant. The goal of the call: leave with one recurring workflow described well enough to write a one-page brief. Capture the trigger, cadence, actors, tools in order, top two failure points, what they have tried, and their words for "fixed". Do not pitch on the call.
- "No" reply: mark closed. Do not follow up.
- After the call: send the brief within 24 hours. Then deliver every other promise made on the call, in the order promised.

## Step 7: follow-up

One follow-up per person, five business days after the first send, and only with something new: a link to the first aggregated finding, or the sample brief if the first email did not include it. Then stop. Two touches total.

## Results from the run

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Emails sent | 20, in two cohorts of 10 (31 Aug and 1 Sep 2026) |
| Replies from cohort 1 inside 18 hours | 1 |
| Calls booked from that reply | 1, for the next morning |
| Sent after the call | Brief, themes memo, this skill |

Plan on one reply in ten for a first cohort with a real research question.

## If the user is selling, not researching

The method still works when the user wants clients, on one condition: the research is real and the brief gets delivered. Example: a fractional product leader who wants engagements can research how product teams cover a PM's leave, interview ten heads of product, send each a brief, and be the person they call when a leave happens. The sale comes on the relationship's timeline, often years for fractional work. Do not pitch on the research call. The participant will hear it as a cold pitch with extra steps.
